How the inflation-adjusted returns calculator works

Your nominal return is what your portfolio shows on paper. Your real return is what that growth is actually worth in purchasing power after inflation. If your portfolio returns 7% when inflation runs at 3%, your real return is about 3.9% (simply subtracting gives 4%). This calculator uses the Fisher equation to show both figures side by side: nominal value, real value in today's dollars, and the purchasing power eroded over time.

U.S. CPI inflation averaged roughly 3% a year from 1926 through 2024. The Fed's current target is 2%. Using 3–3.5% as a long-term planning assumption is reasonable. Using 0% ignores inflation entirely and will significantly overstate your real wealth accumulation.

Real rate of return: the formula

Your inflation-adjusted return (the real rate of return) is what your money gains in purchasing power each year. The exact formula, known as the Fisher equation, is:

Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

The quick shortcut, nominal minus inflation, is close when both numbers are small. It always overstates the real return when they're positive, and the gap widens as inflation rises.

A worked example

Say you invest $10,000 at a 7% annual return for 30 years while inflation averages 3%:

$10,000 · 7% nominal · 3% inflation · 30 years
Real rate: 1.07 ÷ 1.03 − 13.88%
Nominal value after 30 years ($10,000 × 1.07³⁰)$76,123
Value in today's dollars ($76,123 ÷ 1.03³⁰)$31,361
Same result compounding the real rate ($10,000 × 1.0388³⁰)$31,361
Using the 4% shortcut instead ($10,000 × 1.04³⁰)$32,434 — overstated by $1,073

The account shows $76,123, but only $31,361 of that is real growth in purchasing power. Deflating the nominal total and compounding the real rate give the same answer, which is a good check that the formula is right. The shortcut error grows with inflation. At 10% nominal and 8% inflation, the real return is 1.85%, not 2%. That 0.15-point gap matters over decades.

What a real return still leaves out

Taxes are charged on nominal gains. If 7% returns are taxed at 15%, you keep 5.95% nominal, and your after-tax real return is (1.0595 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = 2.86%, not 3.88%. Inflation effectively raises your tax rate on real gains, which is a strong case for tax-advantaged accounts.

Your inflation isn't the CPI. The CPI tracks an average basket. Retirees who spend heavily on healthcare, or renters in high-cost cities, often face higher personal inflation than the headline number.

Averages hide sequences. A steady 3% assumption smooths over bursts like 2021–2022, when year-over-year CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022. A few high-inflation years early in retirement do more damage than the long-run average suggests.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between real and nominal return?

Nominal return is the percentage your account balance grows, the number your brokerage statement shows. Real return is that growth adjusted for inflation: how much more you can actually buy. A 7% nominal return with 3% inflation is a 3.88% real return. Over long periods, real return is the number that matters for goals like retirement, because future expenses rise with prices.

Why not just subtract inflation from my return?

Subtracting (nominal − inflation) is a quick approximation, but it ignores that inflation also erodes your gains, not just your principal. The exact formula is (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. At 7% and 3%, the shortcut gives 4.00% versus the true 3.88%. At 10% and 8%, it gives 2.00% versus 1.85%. The higher inflation runs, the more the shortcut overstates your real growth.

What inflation rate should I use for planning?

U.S. CPI inflation has averaged roughly 3% a year since 1926, and the Federal Reserve targets 2%. Many planners use 2.5–3% for long-term projections and test a higher rate, such as 4–5%, to see how a plan holds up if inflation runs hot. Using 0% will significantly overstate what your future balance can buy.

Can a real return be negative?

Yes. Whenever inflation is higher than your nominal return, you lose purchasing power even though your balance grows. Cash earning 4% during 5% inflation has a real return of (1.04 ÷ 1.05) − 1 = −0.95%. This happened to many savings accounts and bonds in 2021–2022, when inflation outpaced yields.
