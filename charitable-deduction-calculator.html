How 2026 charitable deductions work

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act changed charitable deductions from tax year 2026 on. There are now two separate paths, and you get one or the other:

  • You take the standard deduction ($16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household). On top of it you can deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 joint) of cash gifts to public charities. Gifts to donor-advised funds and supporting organizations don't count. This deduction lowers taxable income, not AGI. Anything over the cap is simply lost: it doesn't carry forward.
  • You itemize. All your qualifying gifts count, but only the part above 0.5% of your AGI. Cash gifts to public charities are still limited to 60% of AGI, and that limit is now permanent. In the 37% bracket, your total itemized deductions are cut by 2/37, which caps their value at about 35 cents per dollar.

The calculator runs both paths and picks the larger deduction. Then it works out how much tax your gifts save compared with giving nothing. That figure is what your generosity really costs or saves you.

A married couple filing jointly, with $150,000 AGI and $24,000 of other itemized deductions (mortgage interest plus state and local taxes). They plan to give $10,000 in cash to their church and a food bank, and they're in the 22% bracket. These are the calculator's default inputs.

Tradeoffs: who wins and who loses under the new rules

Small itemizers lose the most

If your itemized deductions land just above the standard deduction, you take a double hit. The 0.5% floor removes the first slice of your giving, and by itemizing you give up the $1,000/$2,000 non-itemizer deduction. Couple in the example: if they itemized $33,250, they'd beat the plain standard deduction by $1,050 but lose to standard-plus-$2,000 by $950. For many households, the new deduction makes the standard deduction even harder to beat. The calculator flags it when you're in this zone.

Bunching and donor-advised funds

Bunching means you give two (or more) years of gifts in one year, itemize that year, and take the standard deduction plus the non-itemizer deduction in the off years. It works best when your other itemized deductions sit close to the standard deduction. It also beats the floor in a second way: the floor applies once per year, so giving everything in one year means you lose only one year's 0.5%. A donor-advised fund (DAF) makes bunching practical. You deduct the big contribution in the year you make it, then send grants to charities over the following years, so they still get steady support. One catch: DAF contributions only count when you itemize. They never qualify for the $1,000/$2,000 deduction.

What doesn't qualify

  • For the non-itemizer deduction: anything except cash to a public charity. That rules out gifts to donor-advised funds, supporting organizations and most private foundations, gifts of stock or other property, and carryovers from prior years.
  • For anyone: gifts to individuals, political campaigns or GoFundMe-style personal fundraisers, and the part of a payment that buys you something (gala dinner, auction item, event tickets). The value of your time isn't deductible either.
  • Without records: you can't deduct a cash gift of any size without a bank record or written acknowledgment from the charity. For $250 or more you need a written acknowledgment. See IRS Publication 526.

Give before December 31

Gifts count in the year you make them, not the year you pledge or when the charity cashes the check. According to IRS Publication 526, a check counts on the day you mail it, and a credit-card gift counts in the year you charge it, even if you pay the bill in January. If bunching wins for you this year, the deadline that matters is December 31. Online payments started on the last day of the year can post late, so don't cut it close.

What this calculator doesn't model

It treats all income as ordinary income, so it leaves out capital gains rates and the effect of giving appreciated stock (often better than cash, but only if you itemize). It ignores state taxes, other deductions you get whether or not you itemize, and carryforwards from earlier years. If your gifts go over 60% of AGI, the calculator shows the carryforward amount, but it doesn't model using it in future years.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take the $1,000 charitable deduction and the standard deduction in 2026?

Yes. Starting with tax year 2026, if you don't itemize you can deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 married filing jointly) of qualifying cash gifts on top of the standard deduction. It reduces taxable income, not adjusted gross income, so it won't lower AGI-based figures such as IRMAA or credit phaseouts. Anything above the cap isn't deductible and doesn't carry forward.

Do donor-advised fund contributions count for the non-itemizer deduction?

No. The law limits the non-itemizer deduction to cash gifts to public charities. It excludes donor-advised funds and supporting organizations. Gifts of stock or other property don't qualify either. DAF contributions are still deductible if you itemize, which is why DAFs pair well with bunching.

How does the 0.5% AGI floor on charitable deductions work?

Starting in 2026, itemizers can deduct charitable gifts only to the extent the year's total exceeds 0.5% of their contribution base, which is AGI before any net operating loss carryback. With $150,000 of AGI, the first $750 of gifts isn't deductible. With $400,000, it's the first $2,000. The floor doesn't apply to the $1,000/$2,000 non-itemizer deduction. The floored amount carries forward only in a year when your gifts also exceed the AGI percentage limits.

Is my charitable deduction worth 37% if I'm in the top bracket?

Not fully. Starting in 2026, new IRC section 68 reduces itemized deductions by 2/37 of the smaller of two amounts: your total itemized deductions, or how far your income reaches into the 37% bracket. If your income is fully in the top bracket, each deducted dollar saves about 35 cents instead of 37. If you're only partly in the 37% bracket, the reduction is smaller. The calculator applies it automatically.

Does a donation made on December 31 count for this tax year?

Yes, if it's delivered by December 31. According to IRS Publication 526, a mailed check counts on the date you mail it, and a credit-card gift counts in the year the charge is made, even if you pay the bill in January. Pledges don't count until paid.
