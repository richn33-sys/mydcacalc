Average net worth by age — median vs mean, 2025 Fed data

The Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, published October 9, 2026, puts the median American family's net worth at $215,920 and the mean at $1,241,500. Those two numbers measure the same families, yet one is almost six times the other. If you want to know how your net worth compares for your age, which of the two you use matters more than anything else on this page.

Net worth by age: the 2025 Fed numbers

The Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) is the Federal Reserve's survey of household balance sheets, run every three years. It is the standard source for net worth by age because it deliberately oversamples wealthy households, so the top of the distribution is captured properly. The figures below come from Table 2 of the Fed's 2025 report. They are in 2025 dollars, and the age is that of the family's reference person (usually the primary earner).

Median vs mean net worth by age, 2025 SCF ($ thousands, 2025 dollars)

The usual pattern holds. Net worth climbs steeply through the working years as people pay down mortgages and retirement accounts compound. It then levels off around retirement. The median rises about 2.6 times between the 35–44 and 55–64 brackets, from $155,620 to $411,910.

Why median is the better benchmark

The median is the family in the middle: half of families have more and half have less. The mean is total wealth divided by the number of families. Wealth is extremely concentrated, so a small number of very rich households pull the mean far above what a typical family holds.

The Fed's own table shows how lopsided it is. Families in the top 10% by usual income have a median net worth of $3,669,850, about 17 times the overall median. Because of families like these, the mean for all families ($1,241,500) is about three times the median for the 55–64 bracket ($411,910). Most families are nowhere near the mean, and very few are at it.

Rule of thumb

Use the median to answer "how do I compare to a typical household my age?" Use the mean only to answer "how much wealth exists in this age group in total?" If you compare yourself with the mean, you will feel behind even when you are well ahead of most of your peers.

What changed since 2022, and what didn't

The Fed reports both surveys in 2025 dollars, so the comparison below already accounts for inflation. Overall, the median rose 2.3% in real terms, from $211,140 to $215,920. Two age brackets moved a lot, and both need a caveat.

Age2022 median2025 medianReal change
Under 35$42,720$32,970−22.8%
35–44$148,350$155,620+4.9%
45–54$270,560$253,650−6.2%

75 and older$367,250$504,900+37.5%

The SCF is a sample of a few thousand families, and the Fed publishes a standard error for every figure. For the under-35 median, the standard error is $4,100 in 2025 and $5,170 in 2022. A rough significance check using those errors gives a z-score of about −1.5, which falls short of the usual 95% confidence threshold. The decline may be real, but this survey alone doesn't prove it. The 75+ jump is similar (z ≈ 1.9). The 2025 median of $504,900 has a standard error of $65,000, so its 95% range runs from roughly $378,000 to $632,000. That range overlaps the 65–74 bracket, so the data does not show that people aged 75+ are now clearly wealthier than people aged 65–74.

Don't over-read one survey

Each figure here is an estimate with a margin of error. Most single-bracket changes in this table, including the 23% drop for under-35s, are within that margin. Wait for a second survey to confirm a trend before acting on it.

Compared with the 2025 benchmarks for ages 35–44:

  • Against the median ($155,620): $297,500 ÷ $155,620 = 1.91×. This household has nearly twice the net worth of a typical family its age.
  • Against the mean ($681,910): $297,500 ÷ $681,910 = 44%. If you compare with the mean, the same household looks like it's less than halfway there.

The $297,500 is the right total, but where it sits matters too. Here is the same figure split by source:

About 39% of this household's net worth is home equity ($117,000 ÷ $297,500), and another 50% is in retirement accounts. Only $23,500 ($36,000 minus the $12,500 of unsecured debt) can be reached without selling the house, borrowing against it, or paying an early-withdrawal penalty. That is about 3½ months of the 2025 median family income ($82,160 before tax). The household is ahead of the median on paper but has a thin cash cushion. One number can't show both facts. What net worth leaves out, and why averages mislead

Home equity is not spendable money

The SCF counts your home's value minus the mortgage, and for most middle-class families home equity is the largest single asset. But you can't buy groceries with home equity unless you sell, downsize, or take on a new loan. Two households with the same net worth can be in very different positions: one has $300,000 mostly in a house, the other has $300,000 in a brokerage account.

Pensions and Social Security are excluded

The Fed's net worth measure includes balances in 401(k)s, IRAs and similar accounts. It does not include the value of traditional defined-benefit pensions or future Social Security benefits. A retired teacher with a $55,000-a-year pension may show a modest net worth. Yet producing that income for 25 years at a 5% return would take about $775,000. A retiree who funds the same income from a 401(k) shows that $775,000 on paper. Comparing by net worth alone undervalues people with pensions, and this matters most for older age brackets and public-sector workers. Our Social Security estimator helps fill in the part net worth leaves out.

The table compares families, not individuals

The SCF unit is the family. A married couple's combined net worth counts once, under the age of the reference person. A single 40-year-old comparing personal net worth with the 35–44 median is comparing one person with what is often two earners. The Fed's headline table doesn't break net worth down by age and marital status together. If you're single, treat the age benchmark as a high bar.

It's a snapshot of different people, not one life path

The table compares today's 40-year-olds with today's 65-year-olds. It doesn't show what today's 40-year-olds will have at 65. Each generation faces different house prices, interest rates and market returns. The current 65–74 bracket built wealth through decades of falling interest rates and rising asset prices, and that may not repeat.

Comparing yourself with averages can push you into bad decisions

Feeling "behind" the mean leads some people to take on concentrated or leveraged risk to catch up. Feeling "ahead" of the median can lead to under-saving. Neither benchmark knows your income, cost of living, or retirement plan. The most useful comparison is with your own net worth a year ago, plus a target based on what you'll actually spend in retirement. Frequently asked questions

What is the median net worth by age in the US?

According to the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, median family net worth is $32,970 for families headed by someone under 35, $155,620 for ages 35–44, $253,650 for 45–54, $411,910 for 55–64, $431,320 for 65–74, and $504,900 for 75 and older. The median across all families is $215,920. All figures are in 2025 dollars.

What is the average net worth of a 40-year-old?

For families headed by someone aged 35–44, the 2025 SCF reports a median net worth of $155,620 and a mean (average) of $681,910. The median is the better benchmark for a typical household, because the mean is pulled up by a small number of very wealthy families.

Why is average net worth so much higher than median net worth?

Wealth is highly concentrated. The mean adds up everyone's wealth and divides by the number of families, so a few households worth tens or hundreds of millions raise it a lot. The median is simply the middle family and isn't affected by extreme values. In the 2025 survey the mean for all families was 5.8 times the median ($1,241,500 vs $215,920).

Does net worth include my home and retirement accounts?

Yes. The Federal Reserve's measure includes your home's value minus the mortgage, vehicles, business interests, and retirement accounts such as 401(k)s and IRAs. It excludes the value of defined-benefit pensions and future Social Security benefits. If you have a pension, your financial security is higher than your net worth alone suggests.

How do I figure out what my net worth is?

Add up the current market value of everything you own (cash, investment and retirement accounts, home, vehicles, and other assets), then subtract everything you owe (mortgage, auto loans, student loans, credit cards, and other debt). The result is your net worth. The MyDCACalc net worth calculator does the math, shows how much of your net worth is liquid, and compares your result with the 2025 Fed median for your age group.
